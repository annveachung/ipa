Every sound a human voice can make

An interactive guide to the standard IPA chart. Pick a term to see where it sits on the chart, click any symbol to hear the official recording and learn how it's made, then test your ear.

<section>01</section>

Consonants (pulmonic)

Pulmonic consonants use air pushed out from the lungs. Each one is described by three things: where the airflow is blocked (place), how it's blocked (manner), and whether the vocal folds vibrate (voicing). Choose a term to light it up on the chart.

Symbols to the right in a cell are voiced, to the left are voiceless. Shaded areas denote articulations judged impossible.

<section>02</section>

Vowels

Vowels are made with an open vocal tract. The chart is a map of the mouth: up and down is height (how high the tongue is), left to right is backness (front of the mouth on the left), and pairs show rounding of the lips.

Where symbols appear in pairs, the one to the right represents a rounded vowel.

<section>03</section>

Other consonants

Not every consonant uses air from the lungs. Clicks, implosives and ejectives move air with the tongue or the larynx instead. A few more sounds, like double articulations, don't fit the main grid.

<section>04</section>

Diacritics

Diacritics are small marks added to a symbol to show a finer detail: a t with a puff of air becomes tʰ, a nasal vowel ẽ. The dotted circle ◌ stands for any base symbol. Some diacritics may be placed above a symbol with a descender, e.g. ŋ̊.

<section>05</section>

Stress, length & tone

Suprasegmentals describe features that stretch over more than one sound, like stress and length. Tones use pitch to tell words apart; write them as accent marks over a vowel or as Chao tone letters after the syllable.
